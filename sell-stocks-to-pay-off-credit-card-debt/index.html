Should I sell stocks to pay off credit card debt?

Short answer: Stocks have historically returned around 7-10% a year on average, while credit cards often charge 20-30%. Over most realistic periods the card wins that race, so selling taxable investments to clear high-APR cards is usually the better math. Retirement accounts are different: early withdrawals carry taxes and penalties.

Expected return vs guaranteed cost

Paying off a 22% card is like earning a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return. Very few investments promise that. Keeping stocks while carrying the card means betting the market beats 22% this year, which it rarely does.

Example (made-up numbers)

$15,000 in a taxable index fund, $8,000 on a 22% card. Selling $8,000 (plus enough to cover any tax on gains) clears the card and saves around $1,800 a year in interest. Keeping it all invested only wins if the fund gains more than that on the whole $15,000 position after tax.

Before you sell

  • Taxable vs retirement accounts. Selling in a taxable brokerage account may create capital gains. Withdrawing from a 401(k) or IRA early usually triggers income tax plus a 10% penalty (US) and permanently loses tax-advantaged room.
  • Sell losers first where possible: losses can offset gains.
  • Keep an emergency buffer so the card doesn't fill straight back up.
  • Stop the leak. Paying off a card you keep charging only resets the clock.

Common questions

Is it better to invest or pay off credit card debt?

With card APRs of 20%+, paying off the card usually beats investing because it's a guaranteed return. An employer 401(k) match is a common exception worth keeping.

Should I cash out my 401(k) to pay off credit cards?

Usually not without serious thought: early withdrawals can cost income tax plus a 10% penalty and lost growth. Taxable investments or cash are usually better sources.

Do I pay taxes when I sell stocks to pay debt?

Selling in a taxable account at a gain usually creates capital gains tax; held over a year is typically taxed at a lower rate in the US.
